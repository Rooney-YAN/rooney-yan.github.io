*Originally written in Chinese.*

I have said many things I didn't mean in everyday conversations.

I don't mean pretending to agree when I actually disagree. I mean something quieter: I didn't really understand what the other person was saying, but I nodded anyway. Sometimes a conversation reaches a point where the other person's tone is already waiting for an answer, while I am still trying to work out exactly what they meant. But I smile, or say, “Right, right,” and the conversation moves on.

This troubled me for a long time. I thought it was a kind of social laziness, a reluctance to look more closely. Eventually I realized it might be the opposite. I care so much about the other person's feelings that I don't want to ask. Asking would mean taking their words apart, and somewhere in that process I would inevitably find a difference between what they meant and what I heard. Even “What do you mean?” can sound faintly hostile to me. I can't bring myself to say it. So I let the moment pass.

Then there is a second thing.

It isn't only that I don't press other people to explain themselves. What I say often changes shape when it reaches them, too. Sometimes the emotional direction of an entire sentence is reversed. Sometimes a word that felt light to me lands heavily with someone else. More often it is a kind of “context mismatch” that is hard to describe: the same sentence means A in the situation in which I say it and B in the situation in which the other person hears it. It can take us a long time to notice. Sometimes we never do.

Taken together, these two experiences made me wonder what was going on beneath them.

---

Start with language itself.

Language is our main tool for expression, but it has never been a precise one. The meaning of a word comes from shared use, not from a perfectly fixed definition. “Freedom” carries very different things in different contexts, traditions, and mouths. Philosophers can spend centuries arguing over the meaning of a word. In everyday conversation, we use such words constantly to carry meanings we think are clear.

The harder problem is a paradox built into language. The more clearly you want to explain something, the more words you need. Every additional word brings another unit that the listener has to understand accurately. To explain one word, you need others, and each of those has its own blurred edges. The process has no final stopping point. You can never make a thought completely airtight through language alone.

Wittgenstein wrote that some things cannot be put into words, and that we must remain silent about what cannot be spoken of. I don't think this belongs only to remote metaphysical questions. It also speaks to the feelings, judgments, and understandings we try to pass on in ordinary life but never manage to convey in full.

This limitation of language is the first source of misunderstanding, and perhaps the deepest one.

But people do not speak in a vacuum. Social context makes the problem more complicated.

East Asian societies are often described by linguists and anthropologists as “high-context cultures.” The term comes from anthropologist Edward T. Hall. In such cultures, a great deal of information is carried not by the words themselves but by the background, relationship, and situation shared by speaker and listener. Only part of a thought is said aloud; the rest is left for the listener to infer.

Think of expressions like *bu haoyisi* (“sorry” or “excuse me”) and *chabuduo* (“roughly,” “close enough”). What they actually mean depends heavily on who says them, where, how loudly, and whether the speaker looks away or meets your eyes. Pulled out of their setting, they have almost no fixed meaning.

Growing up in such a culture can make you highly dependent on context, and highly sensitive to shifts in it. The trouble is that contemporary life gives us an enormous number of contexts to move between.

Every day we switch roles. In a work chat, you are an employee. In a family chat, you are someone's child. On social media, you may be presenting the person you want others to see. In a small private group, you can say things you would never say elsewhere. Each setting has its own rules, tacit agreements, and vocabulary. In a work conversation, “We can do that” might be a very gentle refusal. Said to a friend, the same words might be a real promise.

The more contexts we inhabit, the more opportunities there are for them to slip out of alignment. Two people may believe they are speaking the same language while carrying different versions of the background code.

Put these three layers together—the imprecision of language, the reliance on inference in high-context cultures, and the fragmentation of modern life—and misunderstanding stops looking like a rare accident. It begins to look like an outcome the system is bound to produce.

That led me to a conclusion I found a little dispiriting: being misunderstood is probably a fate no one who tries to express themselves can escape.

---

The conclusion itself did not surprise me very much. What kept me thinking was the question that followed: if misunderstanding cannot be eliminated, would eliminating it even be a good thing?

It sounds like a strange question. We usually treat misunderstanding as a fault to be repaired, a failure in the communication system between two people. If every expression could be received exactly as intended, with no meaning lost along the way, wouldn't we choose that without hesitation?

After recently watching Neon Genesis Evangelion, I am no longer sure.

In Neon Genesis Evangelion, there is a plan called the Human Instrumentality Project. Its premise is to dissolve humanity into a liquid called LCL, erase the boundaries between individuals, and merge every consciousness into one vast, all-encompassing whole. There would be no misunderstanding, no distance, no loneliness. Everyone would understand everyone else completely and forever—because there would no longer be an “everyone else.”

The plan is driven by Gendo Ikari, a man who has lost the person he loves and cannot bear the enduring separation between people. In a way, he takes the human desire to be understood to its logical extreme. If meaning is always lost between expression and reception, why not erase the distance between them altogether?

The protagonist, Shinji Ikari, is a boy who desperately wants to be understood and accepted. For much of the story, he lives with the pain of feeling that he is neither. He wants to get close to people but is afraid to. He longs for warmth and does not know how to receive it. If anyone ought to accept Instrumentality without hesitation, it would be him.

But at the end of the original theatrical film, Shinji refuses it.

At the moment when people's minds have already begun to merge, he chooses to bring the world back. The boundaries between people return. Each person can once again be a separate individual, with limits, and with the possibility of being misunderstood.

The first time I saw that ending, it shook me in a way I could not quite explain. It was not only the story. It touched a question I had felt for a long time without ever thinking through clearly.

From the standpoint of efficiency, Shinji's choice is irrational. If Instrumentality could really work, it would solve more than misunderstanding. It would solve every problem born of the distance between people: war, prejudice, loneliness, betrayal, all the harm that begins with “I don't know what you're thinking.” From a broad view of humanity, it looks almost like a perfect solution.

It made me think of the Tower of Babel in the Bible. In that story, humanity begins with a single language and decides to build a tower to heaven. God sees that, united in language and purpose, people might be able to do anything. So he confuses their speech, and the building stops. Humanity is left with what feels like a curse: a world in which people can no longer understand one another completely.

Sometimes I wonder what the world would have been like if that curse had never come, if the tower had been completed. It would probably have been extraordinarily efficient. Everyone would speak the same language and think in much the same way. Nothing would be lost in translation; there would be no cultural divide and no misreading caused by different backgrounds. Humanity would become one immense, precise machine, each person a smoothly running part.

But that machine would have no Borges, no Kawabata, no singular person writing late at night for the few readers who might understand. It would have no encounter between people from entirely different worlds in which one hears an idea they had never imagined and feels a strange jolt of recognition. It would lose the creativity that can come from misunderstanding itself: two signals meeting and producing a third wave neither sender expected.

Shinji rejects more than an efficient arrangement for humanity. He rejects understanding purchased through sameness, and the end of loneliness purchased through the end of individuality. He would rather return to a world where he can be misunderstood, hurt, and alone than live in one where everyone understands everyone else completely, but no one remains an independent person.

There is something deeply honest in that choice.

It recognizes that our desire to be understood is not really a desire for the flawless transfer of information. It is a desire for another independent person, with all their own complexity intact, to choose to cross some of the distance between us. If someone understands me only because we have become the same thing, that understanding loses its meaning. Being understood is precious precisely because it happens between two people with boundaries. The distance is what gives the effort weight.

In that sense, understanding and misunderstanding are two sides of the same coin. We cannot keep one and discard the other. Eliminate the possibility of misunderstanding, and we eliminate the condition that makes understanding possible. Diverse cultures, independent personalities, and the beautiful accidents that arise when two different people meet are all evidence of that condition.

Shinji gives the world back to humanity, and with it, the possibility of misunderstanding.

---

So misunderstanding cannot—and should not—be eliminated. What is a person trying to express themselves supposed to do with that?

I want to begin with one distinction: accepting that something is inevitable is different from giving up.

Knowing that expression always loses something along the way does not mean we can say anything carelessly, or stop speaking altogether. If anything, that knowledge asks us to take expression more seriously. Knowing that a letter might be lost in the mail is no reason to stop writing it. It is a reason to write what matters with greater care.

There is an easy nihilistic trap here: if misunderstanding is inevitable, expression is meaningless, and silence is the only honest choice. Wittgenstein's line can certainly be misread that way. But he was speaking of what *cannot* be said, not what is merely *difficult* to say. Most of what we struggle to express in everyday life is difficult, not impossible. Within that space, expression still matters, even if it reaches someone only in part.

The more honest approach, I think, is to treat expression as a gift rather than a transaction.

A transaction works like this: I have offered my words, so you owe me an exact understanding. If you receive them differently, the exchange has failed and both of us have lost something. That way of thinking leaves the speaker in constant anxiety—Did I explain it clearly? Did they understand? Should I add more?—and puts a quiet pressure on the listener, as though getting it wrong were a personal failure.

A gift works differently. It is offered without demanding an equivalent return. I say these things, I write these words, because I genuinely want to, not because I am waiting to be paid back with perfect understanding. There is a kind of lightness in this position. From the beginning, it does not make being fully understood the measure of whether expression has succeeded.

That does not mean understanding is unimportant. It is still one of the things we long for most. But I want to separate the desire to be understood from the demand to be understood. The first is human; it gives expression warmth. The second can become a fixation that turns every misunderstanding into an offense.

Something else is easy to miss: sometimes misunderstanding creates meaning of its own.

A reader may find in a poem something the poet never intended. That need not be a loss. Sometimes the meaning a reader discovers is more interesting, more far-reaching, or more moving to someone than what the poet had in mind. Art is rarely received exactly as its maker imagined. People say there are as many Hamlets as there are readers. The point is not only that interpretations differ. Their divergence is part of what makes art rich. If every poem had just one correct reading, poetry would be a poorer world.

Of course, misunderstandings in daily life are not always beautiful. Many cause pain and friction and need to be repaired. I only mean that misunderstanding, taken as a whole, is not pure loss. At times it is soil in which something can grow that would never have grown in the original context.

---

So, back to the question I started with: are we destined to be misunderstood?

Yes, probably.

But fate need not be only tragic. In Greek myth, Sisyphus is condemned to push a boulder uphill forever. Camus says we must imagine him happy. There is absurdity in that thought, but also clarity. Anyone trying to express themselves is a little like Sisyphus: you know the stone will roll back down. You know that what you say will never arrive perfectly. Still, you push. Still, you speak. Still, you write.

You continue not because you are unaware of this fate, but because you know it and choose to continue anyway.

That is an important difference. One way is mere momentum: you keep going because you have not noticed the difficulty. The other is to see all the limits clearly and still believe the effort is worth making.

I would rather choose the second.
